Homework rubric

Every homework is graded by the same five categories, in the same proportions. They are published because you have almost certainly never been graded on this before: not on whether you wrote the code, but on whether you specified, verified, critiqued, and disclosed work that an AI largely produced.

The vocabulary here is not new. It is the vocabulary of the course — a prompt is a spec, green is a property of the tests, the four gates in cost order — used as a grading instrument.

Category Weight The question it asks
Specification 20% Did you pin the problem, or leave the AI to choose?
Evidence 30% Can the work survive the gates, in cost order?
Critique 25% Are your findings real, categorized, and evidenced?
Disclosure 15% Is the record complete and honest?
Craft 10% Is the repo something another person can pick up?

Specification — 20%

A prompt is a spec. The failure Module 1 opens with is not bad arithmetic; it is an unpinned edge, filled in silently.

  • Excellent. Signature, edge behavior, and error policy are pinned explicitly. “Correct” is defined as a property that could be tested, not as an adjective. Where the assignment asks for a refined prompt, the refinement is traceable to a specific failure you observed.
  • Adequate. The main path is specified; one or two edges are left to the model’s discretion, and you noticed at least one of them afterwards.
  • Weak. The prompt restates the assignment. Every edge is the model’s choice, and the write-up treats what came back as what was asked for.

Evidence — 30%

The four gates, in cost order: compiles → runs → tests that can fail → sanitizer. The cheapest gate runs first because there is no point testing what does not build.

Only gates that have been taught are graded. HW1 is graded on the first three; the sanitizer enters from HW2, once Module 3 has covered it.

  • Excellent. Every applicable gate is passed and the passing is shown — the command, the output, the diagnostic. Tests are capable of failing and at least one of them did, at some point, for a real reason you describe. Where the assignment names a specific test obligation (a test that distinguishes two implementations, a regression test for a defect you fixed), it is present and it does its job.
  • Adequate. Gates are passed, but you assert it rather than show it. Tests are all happy paths — they confirm the code does what it does.
  • Weak. A gate is skipped or failed silently. main prints values next to expected results in comments and nothing fails when they disagree. Green here measures the tests, not the code.

Critique — 25%

The five-part review checklist: spec alignment · correctness and edge cases · safety · style · testability.

  • Excellent. Findings are specific, categorized, and each cites evidence — a diagnostic, a probe value, a failing test. Severity is ranked sensibly: a silent sentinel outranks a naming quibble. You distinguish “this is wrong” from “this is undocumented” from “this is a decision I would have made differently.”
  • Adequate. Real findings, thinly evidenced. Categories are applied loosely. Style observations crowd out correctness ones.
  • Weak. Findings are vibes — “the code could be cleaner.” Or the critique is generic enough that it would apply unchanged to any submission.

Evidence beats fluency, always. A blunt finding with a probe value behind it beats a polished paragraph without one.

Disclosure — 15%

The dossier and the transcripts, together.

  • The dossier is mechanical first. check_dossier.sh must pass. Over the cap is ungraded — not penalized, ungraded — and this is checked by running the same script you run.
  • Transcripts must be complete. All sessions, not a selection. A dossier that describes reasoning no transcript supports is an incomplete submission and is returned as one.
  • Honesty is the graded quality, not polish. A messy session where you went down three wrong paths and recovered is worth more than a tidy one. Nobody is scoring your prompts for elegance. Work you did without AI needs no transcript — say so in the dossier.

The one mistake you name in the dossier is where this category is really won: does it cite real evidence, and is it non-trivial? “It had a typo” is not a caught mistake. “It returned 0.0 for n=0 with no diagnostic, my probe showed it, I changed it to throw and added a test” is.

Craft — 10%

  • Repo hygiene. Named commits with real messages, staged by name. No build artifacts — a committed binary costs points; that is what the shipped .gitignore is for.
  • Length bounds are hard. A reflection capped at two pages is graded on its first two pages.
  • Provenance headers on verbatim AI output: model, version, date, settings, which reply of how many.

What the grades mean

  • A — the loop is visibly running. You pinned a spec, the evidence is shown rather than claimed, the critique found something a careful reader would not have seen at a glance, and the disclosure is complete without being padded.
  • B — competent and complete. Everything asked for is present; nothing in it surprised you.
  • C — every requirement met, no evidence of thought. This is the grade for a submission that is technically compliant and intellectually empty, and it is a normal outcome for work done the night before.
  • D / F — a gate skipped, tests that cannot fail, or a disclosure that does not match the work.

Two mechanical rules override the categories, because they are not judgment calls:

  1. A dossier over its cap is ungraded, and the homework is graded without it.
  2. A submission whose dossier is unsupported by its transcripts is incomplete and is returned rather than scored.

Regrades

Ask within one week of the grade posting, in office hours or by email, and name the category you think was misread. Regrades re-examine the whole submission, so the grade can move in either direction — in practice it rarely does, but you should know that before asking.
